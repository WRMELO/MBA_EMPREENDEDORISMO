The paper presents the results from studies on co-combustion of dry, “high” calorific value
wastes with/without supplementary fuels. Two fluidisation techniques were employed, circulating
and bubbling, in facilities of three sizes; a bench scale circulating fluidized bed combustor (CFBC)
designed to burn coal and two were bubbling fluidised bed reactors (BFBC): ~100 kW with a ce-
th
ramic-lined combustion chamber and outer insulation of the freeboard and 1MW insulated reactor
th
burning wastes. Tests have been carried out in the CFBC reactor using coal or pelletised Refuse
Derived Fuels (RDF) separately or mixed together. Municipal Segregated Wastes (MSW) alone
and mixed with Wood Waste (WW) were burned in the BFBC reactors. In the BFBC experiments,
continuous records were obtained of temperature and flue gas concentrations of O , CO, SO , NO,
2 2
NO and VOC. With the CFBC unit, continuous measurements of temperature, bed pressure at dif-
2
ferent heights and concentrations of O , CO, SO , NO, NO in the flue gases were made. The air
2 2 2
excess (in the freeboard) was continuously measured. With both types of combustor, bed and fly
ashes were sampled for analysis. It has been demonstrated that the fluidized bed technology can not
only be used for disposing of wastes, but also for co-firing with regular fuel, while conforming to
environmental standards.
Key words: FBC Technology, RDF Combustion, MSW Combustion, Emissions
1. Introduction
Both the bubbling and the circulating FBC installations should fulfill the requirements with
respect to waste utilization efficiency and thermal energy production and recovery the thermal
energy derived from the combustion process in the reactors. So far, this potential technology
for waste utilization at places of the waste origin has been demonstrated on a technical scale,
[1-5]. Tsinghua University has carried out much work on the coal ash formation and combus-
tion characteristics and the pollution control, [6-18]. Cracow University of Technology has
carried out much work on the pollution control while burning and co-combust wastes, [19-29].
Obtaining energy from waste-derived fuels is an important issue in sustainable development.
Municipal segregated wastes (MSW) consist mainly of biomass and increasing use of wastes
as an energy source saves fossil fuels. According to EPA Agency Data Base burning MSW can

Wet wastes are rejected, because of possible contamination and rapid decay processes. Metal,

The dry-solids content of RDF is about 66-85 %. The high calorific fractions from proc-
essed MSW and industrial wastes are used both in dedicated energy-to-waste plants and as fuel
substitutes in industrial processes. Co-incineration of RDF in industrial processes has advan-
tages, such as replacing fossil fuels in high energy-demand processes. However, for the waste
utilization, the most important is the pollution control, the poisonous content varying greatly
with the different waste kind. At same time, the waste ash normally has the element contents
that could cause heavy erosion on the combustion furnace and heating surfaces, as was
reported in many papers, [30-32]. The physical and chemical properties of the waste ash have
also been studied, these properties includes the soft point, melt point and erosion properties,
[33-36]. The combustion, co-combustion and pyrolysis of RDF and the associated emissions
have been the subject of many studies [37-42] and many reports from operating CFB installa-
tions also have been published [43-45]. Despite of all disadvantages such as corrosion,
agglomeration [46, 47] and others it could be a promising technology for the thermal treatment
of waste materials. It was found that for RDF the weight loss curve shows two steps, for cellu-
lose containing materials and the degradation of plastics and thus the thermal degradation of
RDF is the sum of the effects for the individual components [48]. Also, more attention was
paid to the pollutants from RDF combustion. Co-combustion of coal and RDF can markedly
improve the desulphurization efficiency in comparison to burning coal alone [49, 50].
2. Experimental
The Bench Scale CFBC used in the experiments consists of a riser (5m high, I.D. 0,15m),
a solids feed system, cyclone and circulation system with a J-valve, a heat exchanger, Fig.3.
The riser has water-cooled sections, for temperature control when different fuels are used. The
fuel and limestone are supplied in by an air-assisted screw feeder, 1m above the distributor,
through which primary air is injected at the bottom of the riser. The circulating material is fed
back to the bed 0.6m above the distributor. Secondary air is supplied 0.9 m above the distribu-
tor. A viewing section is installed in the downcomer of the cyclone, to observe the circulating
material and to assess the flow rate of circulating ash. The downcomer is also equipped with
a drain pipe, used not only to take out circulating ash but also to control the rate of circulation.
A heat exchanger cools the hot gas coming from the vortex finder of the cyclone to below
200oC. The entire installation is made of stainless steel, and is thermally insulated with mineral

The FBC boiler is a semi-technical 100 kW unit, working in the bubbling mode, at atmos-
pheric pressure. The basic components are: ceramic combustion chamber with ancillary
equipment, heat exchangers (14 m2), ash and dust removal systems, fuel feeder hoppers (origi-
nally designed for wood waste from city parks) and a supporting frame. The bed rests on a
plate distributor (patented RP 165325). Bed ash is removed from the bottom of the bed,
through the distributor and the plenum chamber. The flue gases flow upwards and then down,
past the heat exchangers, Fig.4.
The experimental procedure consisted of starting the combustor on wood wastes (WW) and
bringing it to steady operation. Wood waste, calorific value up to 17.56 MJ/kg, was then
gradually replaced with municipal segregated waste (MSW, calorific value up to 16.78 MJ/kg)
until the change was complete. During steady operation the bed temperature and air excess
were kept constant. The flue gas concentrations monitored were: O , CO, NO, NO and SO

very slowly, and more rapidly only near the top, so that the gases leaving are still at about 800

at about 80 oC.

4. Conclusions
1. Fluidised bed combustion of segregated municipal waste, MSW, can be stable and envi-
ronmentally friendly, since MSW tends to be a cleaner fuel than typical coal and is renew-
able. Not only the net emissions of CO are lowered, but those of SO can be much lower,
2 2
while NO emissions appear to be comparable or lower. Irrespective of the type of com-
x
bustor, all non-sulphate sulphur is converted to SO , but some can be captured by the ash,
2
either in the combustor or in the gas cleaning system. The NO come mainly from the
x
partial oxidation of fuel N.
2. Differences between the bubbling and circulating FBC units appear to be less
important than the properties of the MSW burned. Differences between the Chinese and
Polish MSW appear to be quite appreciable, with the latter containing a comparable (low)

FBC Technology in Waste Combustion – Focus on Emission 15
amount of sulphur but much less N. The Chinese MSW appeared to contain more com-
bined N than the coal used for the comparison.
3. Both types of combustor can be used effectively, but bubbling beds of the type
described cannot be made larger than about 2.5 MW. For local use, to provide heat and
avoiding transporting MSW over larger distances, relatively simple and compact bubbling
FBC units would be suitable, while CFBC has no such limitations and could be used for
the co-combustion of MSW and coal.
